# Should you raise at all?

## Purpose

Revenue first is the default, and this stage is optional. This card ends with a written verdict on outside capital, based on the traction you logged in stages 7 and 8. Picture a booking tool for physio clinics at $7k MRR that signs three clinics a week through referrals. A cheque might pay for a sales hire who doubles that pace, or it might only fund salaries while referrals keep bringing in the same three clinics. Your numbers show which story is true. Plenty of founders finish here with "not yet", and that is a finished result.

## When to use

Use this card once stage 8 is done. People pay you, one metric climbs week on week, and you keep hitting a limit that cash would remove. A common trigger: your invoicing tool for agencies has 40 accounts on a waitlist, and you can't onboard them without a second engineer.

This is the wrong card if nobody pays yet or the metric is flat. Go back to stage 7 or 8. It is also the wrong card if you have already decided to raise and want help with the deck. The sibling card 'Pitch in the order investors believe: traction, team, social proof' covers that, and 'What angels screen for' covers individual investors.

## Principles

- Assume customers fund you until proven otherwise. A CRM plugin growing 8% a week on card payments is already doing what a round is supposed to buy.
- Raise only when money is the bottleneck. If users don't stay, a bigger bank balance won't fix it.
- Raise from strength. Graham separates type A fundraising (you would survive without the money) from type B fundraising (you would not). Type A is far easier. Try to reach ramen profitability, his term, first.
- Size the round to the next milestone. Geoff Ralston's seed guide links the amount to a believable plan for roughly the next 12 to 18 months.
- Pitching eats the calendar. Two founders behind a Slack standup bot spend a month on scattered investor coffees and end it with no release shipped and a stack of unanswered churn emails. Graham's fix is fundraising mode, a state you are either in or out of. Squeeze the raise into a few set weeks, stack every meeting inside them, and ship again once they close.

## Procedure

1. Build a single traction sheet from stages 7 and 8. List paying customers, current monthly revenue, the one metric you push, and how that metric changed each week across the past eight weeks. For a B2B onboarding tool it might read: 23 paying teams, $4.1k MRR, activated seats up 7% a week. Michael Seibel says he cares more about progress than clever ideas, so finish the sheet before you open a slide tool. Show: the one-page traction sheet.
2. Name the constraint in one sentence: "We would grow faster if we had X." If money can't buy X (a sharper customer profile, better retention, founder focus), stop and fix that first. Show: the sentence, marked yes or no for "cash buys this".
3. Write the zero-dollar plan. With current revenue and costs, when does the company pay its founders enough to live on? A marketplace for freelance bookkeepers that takes 12% of $30k monthly volume earns $3.6k a month. That plan is slow, but it is real. Show: a runway and break-even sheet with no outside money.
4. Write two funded plans. Pick a milestone an investor would recognise, such as $50k MRR or 200 paying teams. Cost it as headcount times loaded monthly cost times months to get there, plus any large non-staff spend. Make one small plan and one larger one. Show: two costed plans, each tied to a milestone and a date.
5. Put the zero-dollar plan next to the two funded ones and run two checks. Speed: does a round pull the milestone in by months, not weeks? Survival: does the unfunded plan reach break-even before savings run out? Pass both and you are raising from Graham's type A position. The bookkeeping marketplace, for example, gets to ramen profitability alone in about 14 months, while a $600k round puts 200 paying firms on the platform by month 9. Fail the survival check and you are in type B, so cut costs until the zero-dollar plan works and then revisit. Show: "raise" or "not yet" in writing, plus the reason.
6. **If the verdict is raise, decide who first.** Rank every possible investor by two things multiplied together: how likely they are to say yes, and what they bring besides money. For a quoting app sold to commercial cleaning firms, an operator who built a janitorial franchise can outrank a famous fund that has never met a facilities manager. Approach the top of the list together rather than one by one; Graham calls this breadth-first search weighted by expected value. Show: the ranked sheet.

7. **Then decide when, and on what paper.** Block two or three weeks for meetings and write down the last day you will take a new one. Use YC's safe unless you have a reason not to; Ralston's guide explains why it replaced convertible notes for most seed rounds. Get caps, dilution and terms from the guide and the safe primer, not from memory. Show: the meeting window with its hard stop, and the instrument.

## Artifacts produced

- `traction_snapshot`: the stage 7-8 metrics on one page.
- `growth_constraint`: the constraint sentence and whether cash buys it.
- `raise_plans`: the zero-dollar plan and two funded plans, each with a milestone.
- `raise_decision`: raise or not yet, with the reason. If raising, it also records the instrument, the target amount, the ranked investor list and the process dates.

## Anti-patterns

- **Raising because startups raise.** Two batchmates post their rounds on LinkedIn in the same month, and a deck suddenly feels overdue. Ask the founder what the money buys and you hear "growth" or "hiring" with no role named. To spot it: the step 2 sentence is blank or says "to scale".
- **Money for a product problem.** Churn is 15% a month and the plan is to spend on ads. To spot it: in the funded plan, signups go up and retention stays the same.
- **Round sized by headline.** The founder asks for $2M because a peer did. To spot it: there is no milestone, or the milestone doesn't follow from the cost math.
- **One investor at a time.** Weeks of calls with one fund while the product stalls. To spot it: the list has a single active name and no end date.
- **The endless raise.** The founder has been "sort of raising" for three months. To spot it: the weekly metric flattened after the first investor meeting.
- **Holding out on valuation.** The founder turns down a fair safe to wait for a higher cap. Ralston and Graham both rank valuation below getting the money and getting good investors.

## Gate criteria

The `stage-9-fundraise` gate opens only if stages 7 and 8 justify it. From this card, it looks for:

- a traction snapshot showing paying customers and a metric that moved
- a constraint that money can buy
- a zero-dollar plan plus funded plans tied to milestones
- a written decision

A reasoned "not yet" is a complete result. The founder goes back to growing on revenue.
