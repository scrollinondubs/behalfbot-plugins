# Pitch in the order investors believe: traction, team, social proof

## Purpose

A partner decides whether to meet you from an email and possibly one page behind it, before any slides come into play. This card builds both: the elevator pitch (Babak Nivi and Naval Ravikant's term in Pitching Hacks) and a one-page executive summary. When the founder of a warehouse-labelling SaaS gets these two right, they land on a partner's calendar. When they get them wrong, the thread goes quiet.

Every figure in both comes from your stage 7 and 8 ledger. Picture the founder of a dental-clinic booking tool about to email a seed fund: any number they can't trace to an entry gets cut before they hit send.

## When to use

Use it at stage 9, once the sibling card 'Should you raise at all?' has come back yes and stages 7 and 8 left real numbers behind. That means paying customers, retention and a growth rate.

It is the wrong card if the ledger is thin. Polishing a pitch with no traction hides the gap. Go back to stage 7 instead. It is also the wrong card for learning how angels decide (see 'What angels screen for'), for running the meeting itself, or for terms.

## Principles

- Traction goes first. In Pitching Hacks, Nivi and Ravikant treat evidence of real usage as the strongest thing on the page. Claim the highest rung you honestly can. Profit beats revenue, and revenue beats a customer count. Pilots and free users come below that, and proof that customers feel the problem sits at the bottom. The trend matters as well. A scheduling SaaS at $3k MRR growing 12% a month tells a better story than one at $6k that has been flat since spring.
- Team comes second, told as past results rather than job titles. Social proof comes third: who already backs you, advises you or introduced you.
- The product gets one short paragraph. Investors judge it by how customers react to it.
- Warm routes win, and the value of a route depends on history with that specific investor. Pitching Hacks calls the person who carries you in a middleman. A founder selling clinic-scheduling software gets more from an angel who has co-invested with the target twice than from a well-known newsletter writer who once shared a panel with them.
- If a number has no ledger entry behind it, it stays out.

## Procedure

1. **Pull the proof.** Open your stage 7 and 8 ledger entries and list every number worth showing: MRR, paying accounts, retention by cohort, weekly or monthly growth, pilot conversions. Write the ledger id next to each one. Show: a proof list with ids.
2. **Rank it.** Put the strongest traction first, using the ranking above. Say you run a B2B invoice-matching tool with 14 paying finance teams and 9% monthly revenue growth. Open with those numbers, not with how accurate your OCR is. Show: the ranked list.
3. **Write the high-concept pitch.** Nivi and Ravikant use this term for a comparison line: a thing people know, applied somewhere new. A shift-swap app for hospital nurses might go with "Calendly for hospital shift swaps." Set that beside "a workforce-optimization platform for healthcare," which nobody can repeat. Text your line to two friends who don't work in tech, then ask them a day later what your company does. Keep it only if their answers match. Show: the line and both replies.
4. **Draft the email.** Work down the step 2 list and stop around 150 words. The restaurant-approvals founder opens with "23 restaurant groups, $41k MRR, no churn in two quarters" and spends one sentence on the product. Show: the draft, a ledger id beside every number.
5. **Write the one-page executive summary.** Use the same order with more room: problem, customer, a traction table with ledger ids, team, who has already committed, and how much you are raising and which milestones it pays for over the next few quarters. Pitching Hacks itself treats a separate summary as optional once you have a pitch and a deck. FounderOS asks for one anyway, because a single page forces every claim onto an entry the gate can check. Show: the one-pager.
6. **No deck until an investor asks.** Show: the deck, if requested.
7. **List your routes in.** For the freight-quoting seed partner, that might be the CEO of a company they backed and an angel who shared a round with them. Write the shared history in one line each, and ask the closest one for an intro that includes a sentence of praise. Show: the list and the date of your first ask.

## Artifacts produced

- An `elevator_pitch` artifact: the high-concept line and the forwardable email, with a ledger id on every traction claim.
- An `executive_summary` artifact: the one-pager, tagged the same way.
- A `middleman_list` artifact: each target investor, the contact for them, how they know each other, and the status of the ask.
- An optional `deck` artifact.

## Anti-patterns

- **Product first.** The email opens with three paragraphs on features. To spot it, count the words. If the product text is longer than the traction text, reorder.
- **Vanity traction.** "2,000 signups" for a marketplace of freelance editors where only 40 have ever finished a job. To spot it, look for revenue or repeat-use entries behind the number. There are none.
- **Totals with no direction.** A big number with no rate of change. To spot it, search for "per week" or "per month". Neither appears.
- **Cold blast.** Fifty identical emails sent to partner inboxes. To spot it, check the middleman list. It is empty.
- **Weak introducer.** Your contact swapped cards with the partner at a SaaS meetup and offers to "loop them in." To spot it, ask them to name one deal, board or project the two have shared. They can't name one.
- **Titles instead of track record.** The team section reads "CEO, CTO, Head of Growth." To spot it, look for a past result next to each name. There isn't one.
- **Unsourced claim.** A retention figure that appears nowhere in the ledger. To spot it, look for its ledger id. It is missing.

## Gate criteria

The stage-9-fundraise gate opens only if stages 7 and 8 justify it. From this card's work, it looks for:

- an elevator pitch and a one-page executive summary that put traction before team and team before social proof, with the product kept to one short paragraph
- a ledger id from stage 7 or 8 on every number
- a middleman list showing warm routes to named investors

A pitch whose numbers don't match the ledger fails.
