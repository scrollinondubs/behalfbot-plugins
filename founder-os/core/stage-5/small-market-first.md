# Own a small market before a big one

## Purpose

Test that your foothold segment is small enough to win outright. Then write down where you go once you have won it.

## When to use

Use it at stage 5, once the founder has a one-sentence job and a candidate foothold segment. The sibling card 'One sentence for the job, one segment for the foothold' covers how to pick that segment. This card checks whether the segment is the right size and whether the founder can own it.

It is the wrong card if there is no segment yet. Send the founder back to the sibling card. It is also the wrong card for rewriting the launch document. That belongs to 'PR/FAQ v2: rewrite the launch from the evidence'.

## Principles

- Aim for the position Peter Thiel calls **monopoly**. Picture a physio clinic owner who has used your intake tool for a month and would not switch back to paper or to a rival app, because nothing else fits how the clinic runs. Choose a first market small enough to reach that point this year.
- Small helps you. A few hundred buyers you can name are worth more than millions you can only estimate.
- Small still has to be real. The people exist, share the problem and gather somewhere you can reach.
- Being one of many is the trap. Where ten tools look alike, buyers pick on price and margins shrink to nothing.
- Shipping first matters less than lasting. Thiel names this **last mover advantage**: the company still standing when the market settles is the one nobody bothers to replace. For the intake tool, that means winning physios outright, then chiropractors, before going anywhere near general scheduling.

## Procedure

1. **Count the first market in people.** Give the role, the trigger and a number. For example: "412 independent physio clinics in the UK still taking intake on paper forms." Show: the count and where it came from (a directory, a LinkedIn search, a community member list).
2. **Check they cluster.** Find the channels that reach at least half of them, such as one trade association, one marketplace category or one Slack group. Show: the list of channels, with an estimated reach for each.
3. **List who serves them today.** Include spreadsheets, a local agency and "the office manager does it by hand". Show: a competitor table with one line per row saying why a buyer would switch to you.
4. **Test whether you can become the obvious choice.** Picture one clinic owner asking another what to use for intake. Within a year, would the answer be your product? Write down what would have to be true for that. Show: a target share (say 30% of the counted group in 12 months) and the reasoning behind it.
5. **Run the two size-lie checks** from Anti-patterns on your own market sentence. Show: the sentence as it reads after both checks.
6. **Write the expansion sequence.** Name two or three adjacent markets in order. Each one is a short step from the previous one: the same buyer with a new job, or the same job for a neighbouring buyer. Intake for physios, then for chiropractors, then scheduling for both. Show: the ordered list, with one line on why each step is adjacent.
7. **Say what keeps you there.** Write one sentence on what would stop a newcomer from displacing you once you lead, such as data that builds up or a network between users. Show: that sentence.

## Artifacts produced

- A `market_sizing` artifact attached to the foothold segment. It holds the headcount with its source, the reach channels, the competitor table and the target share.
- An `expansion_sequence` artifact: the ordered adjacent markets, with the reason for each step and the one-line durability note.
- A revised market sentence, ready for the PR/FAQ v2.

## Anti-patterns

- **The fake intersection.** "AI invoicing for vegan food trucks that also run catering." Each circle is real, but the overlap might hold nine businesses or none. Spot it: you cannot name ten of them. A narrow market is fine. An imaginary one is not.
- **The sliver of a giant.** "Project management software is a huge market and we only need 1% of it." Spot it: the claim starts with a top-down dollar figure and has no headcount. Big markets have incumbents and no clear way in, so the 1% rarely arrives, and when it does, prices fall to nothing.
- **Small but scattered.** The group is real, but its members share no channel and meet the problem only now and then. Spot it: step 2 finds no channel that reaches more than a handful of them.
- **One of many.** The eleventh Notion template shop, or another freelancer marketplace for designers. Spot it: the competitor table has five or more rows that do the same thing, and your edge is price or a nicer UI.
- **Straight to everyone.** The expansion sequence goes from the niche to "all SMBs" in one step. Spot it: you cannot say why the second market would trust you because of the first.

## Gate criteria

The `stage-5-position-and-story` gate needs a one-sentence job, a foothold segment and a PR/FAQ v2. From this card, the auditor checks that the foothold segment:

- has a headcount with a source
- has named channels that reach most of it
- has a competitor table showing where you would be the obvious choice
- has a target share with its reasoning
- has an ordered expansion sequence into adjacent markets

Any market claim in the PR/FAQ v2 has to use the counted group, not a top-down dollar figure. A claim that fails either size-lie check fails the gate.
