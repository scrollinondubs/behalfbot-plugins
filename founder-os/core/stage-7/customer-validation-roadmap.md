# Customer validation and the sales roadmap

## Purpose

A buyer who complained about their month-end mess in a stage 3 interview has given you a signal. That is not a sale. Take a reconciliation tool for small agencies: nine owners told you closing the books eats two days every month. At stage 7 each of them sees a price and a pilot agreement, and you record what they did next, step by step.

Steve Blank and Bob Dorf call this stage customer validation.

Say two of the nine agency owners sign, and in both firms the bookkeeper pushed for it. Run another round with the same pitch, or rewrite it for bookkeepers and go again? Blank and Dorf call that decision pivot or proceed.

## When to use

This is the stage 7 card. Before you open it, check that you have a working product a buyer can sign into and the [[earlyvangelist]] list you built at stage 3. Earlyvangelist is Blank and Dorf's term for the buyer who feels the pain, has already rigged up a workaround and has budget to fix it now. Think of the agency owner who built a Zapier chain to chase late invoices and asked for your pricing page before it existed.

This is the wrong card if nobody on that list has asked when they can buy. Go back and find stronger prospects first. It is also the wrong card for writing cold emails, which belongs to 'Founder-led outbound'. Practising the price ask inside one call belongs to 'Ask for money: state the price, count the commitments'.

## Principles

- Money or a signature is the only result that counts. Compliments and waitlist signups do not.
- The founder sells. Only the person who can change the product should hear why a deal died.
- Start with earlyvangelists. If the most desperate buyers will not pay, calm strangers will not either.
- Every deal is a data point: who said yes, who slowed it down and how many days it took.
- Show that one sale can be repeated before you pay to repeat it. Bigger ad spend (Blank and Dorf's customer creation), new managers (their company building) and paid reps all wait until the deal log backs them.

## Procedure

1. Write the value proposition in two sentences a buyer could repeat to their boss: who it is for, which painful job it removes and what it costs. Show: the two sentences, dated.
2. Make the smallest useful set of sales material: a one-page explainer, a live walkthrough or short demo video, and a pilot agreement or order form with the price already filled in. Show: the three files.
3. Before the first pitch, write your guess at every hand the deal passes through. For a fleet-maintenance tracker sold to haulage firms: a workshop supervisor logs a month of repairs, the fleet manager asks if downtime fell, procurement sends a security form, the managing director approves. Next to each: what that person needs to see, and the likeliest excuse for waiting. Show: that page, labelled v0.
4. Work the stage 3 earlyvangelist list yourself. Ask each person for an order or a paid pilot at, or close to, your real price. Show: one deal-log row per prospect.
5. For every deal, record the daily user, the person who controls the budget, anyone who blocked or stalled it and why, the date of first contact, the date of the yes or no, and every step in between. Show: the filled deal log.
6. Every fifth closed or lost deal, open the roadmap next to the log and edit it. Delete steps no buyer went through, add the ones nobody predicted, and flag where deals sit longest. If three restaurant groups all stalled at the IT payroll check, that step gets a warning and a plan. Show: roadmap v1, v2 and so on, each with a short changelog.
7. Write the pass bar for your decision before you open the deal log, and date it. For the fleet tracker: "By 30 November, three of eight haulage firms have paid six months upfront, and two have cleared procurement on standard terms." Then score the log against it. Proceed if it cleared. If you pivot, name what changed: the buyer, the price or the route to them. File the decision next to the numbers. Show: the pivot-or-proceed record.

## Artifacts produced

- `value_proposition`: the two sentences, dated.
- `sales_collateral`: the explainer, the demo and the pilot agreement or order form.
- `deal_log`: one row per prospect with roles, blockers, dates, price and outcome.
- `sales_roadmap`: Blank and Dorf's name for the map of who touches a deal between first contact and cash. Numbered versions, each showing who approves what.
- `pivot_or_proceed`: the pass bar and the date it was set, the evidence measured against it, the decision and the next action.

## Anti-patterns

- **Hiring the closer first.** A commission-only rep or an agency comes on while the founder still has zero closes of their own. The sign: ask the founder who approved the latest contract and what tipped it, and they have to check with the rep.
- **The endless free pilot.** Every "customer" is on a free trial with no end date. If no money moved and nothing was signed, it was a conversation, not validation.
- **Custom build creep.** Each buyer gets a one-off feature to close the deal. You can spot it when three customers are running three different versions of the product.
- **Exclusivity for a logo.** A well-known company asks to be the only buyer in its category. Agreeing caps your market before you know how big it is.
- **Moving the bar.** The pass criteria get rewritten after the results come in. You can spot it when the bar in the pivot-or-proceed record has no date, or is dated after the deals.
- **Roadmap from memory.** The roadmap looks like a tidy funnel, but the deal log is thin or has no dates.

## Gate criteria

The `stage-7-validation-and-sales` gate needs paying customers or signed pilots. From this card it looks for:

- Deal-log entries backed by an invoice, a payment or a signed pilot agreement.
- A sales roadmap at v1 or later that matches what the deal log shows.
- A pivot-or-proceed record with a pass bar set in advance and a written decision.

A proceed decision with no paid or signed deals fails.
