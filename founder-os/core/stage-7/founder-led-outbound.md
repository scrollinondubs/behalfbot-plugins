# Founder-led outbound

## Purpose

Win your first paying customers or signed pilots by doing the selling yourself, one named prospect at a time. Keep a record of every attempt so the result can be trusted.

## When to use

Use it at stage 7. By then you should be able to describe the pain in the buyer's own words and have something a buyer could start using or piloting. If nobody on your stage 3 list recognised the problem, outreach will only get you polite silence. Go back to stage 3.

This card does not help you decide whether to pivot. That decision belongs to the sibling card 'Customer validation and the sales roadmap' (Blank). How to name a price and judge whether a yes is real is covered in 'Ask for money: state the price, count the commitments'.

## Principles

- You sell until a written pitch closes deals on its own. A solo founder with an invoice-chasing tool for freelance designers hears on call ten that the real fear is annoying a client they want to keep, not forgetting to send reminders. The landing page changes that afternoon. A contractor paid on commission would have logged "not interested" and moved on.
- A short list you understand beats a long list you bought.
- A cold email is about the reader. Their workflow, their problem, one ask.
- Silence is not an answer. Follow up until you hear yes or no.
- A no is evidence. Log it the same way you log a yes.

## Procedure

1. **Build the list.** Pull 30 to 60 names from your ledger. Start with the people who described the pain in stage 3, then the users, waitlist sign-ups or pilot leads from stage 6. Add lookalikes only where you can name the reason they fit, such as the same role, the same tool or the same complaint. Show: a list with name, role, company, why they fit, and where you found them.

2. **Write the email.** Keep it under 100 words. The first line is about them, the second is what you built, and the last is a single question they can answer in one line. Leave out your funding, your founding story and your features. For a founder selling a maintenance-ticket tool to property managers:

   > Subject: repair requests at Harbor Lane
   >
   > Hi Dana, last month you told me your team copies tenant repair emails into the work-order system by hand. I built a tool that does that step automatically, and two small firms are trying it now. Is a 15-minute call Thursday worth it to see whether it fits Harbor Lane?

   Show: the email text, with a version label.

3. **Send in small batches.** Send 15 to 25 a day, never hundreds at once, so you can follow up with every person who replies. Once the batch has had a couple of days, compare replies against sends. If almost nobody answers, rewrite the first line and the ask before you send more. Show: sends, replies and version for each batch.

4. **Call.** Phone the people who opened but did not reply, and anyone on the list with a public number. In the first breath, say who you are and why you picked them. Ask whether the problem is still live. Book a next step before you hang up. Show: call entries in the log with outcome and next step.

5. **Follow up.** Set a cadence and keep to it. For example, nudge on day 3, add something useful on day 7, try a different channel on day 14, then send a last note asking whether to close the file. Each touch should carry something new. Show: every touch dated in the log, with no prospect left without a status.

6. **Find the route to a yes, then ask for it.** Ask who else has a say before you ask for anything. A dental practice manager piloting your onboarding checklist mentions three: the owner (anything over $200 a month), the IT contractor (patient data), and the finance calendar (new spend waits a quarter). Efti's name for this move is the virtual close. No route at all means not now, with a revisit date.

   Then ask. A warm call that ends without a commitment has produced nothing you can count. Finish every promising call by naming one next step with a day attached: "Shall I set up accounts for your three front-desk staff on Wednesday?" Show: a signed pilot, a payment, or a logged no with the reason.

7. **Close the loop.** Mark every prospect as won, lost, not now (with a date to revisit) or no response after the full cadence. Show: a log with no blank outcomes.

## Artifacts produced

- A `prospect_list` artifact: each name, role, company, fit reason and source stage.
- An `outreach_log` artifact: every touch with its date, channel, message version, outcome and next action. Nos, not-nows and no-responses count as outcomes.
- A `commitment` record for each signed pilot or payment, linked to the log entries that led to it.

## Anti-patterns

- **The bought list.** Most names have no link to your stage 3 or stage 6 evidence, and you cannot say why any one of them fits.
- **The about-us email.** The first sentence mentions your company, your raise or your launch. The prospect's name shows up only in the greeting.
- **One and done.** Most rows in the log have one touch and the status "no reply".
- **Hiding in the inbox.** The log contains hundreds of emails and no calls. Fear of the phone is normal. It shows up in the log as a missing channel.
- **The missing no.** The log shows only wins and warm leads. Cold replies and hang-ups were never written down, so the gate cannot tell a 3-in-10 hit rate from a 3-in-300 one.
- **Selling the roadmap.** You get a yes by promising features that do not exist yet. That customer churns, or the pilot stalls when the feature is not there.
- **Never asking.** Every good call ends with "I'll send some info". Months later, the next action for each prospect is still "follow up".

## Gate criteria

The `stage-7-validation-and-sales` gate looks for paying customers or signed pilots. From this card it expects:

- An outreach log showing every touch and outcome, nos included.
- At least one signed pilot or payment, traceable to specific rows in that log.
- Evidence the founder did the selling: calls and emails sent by the founder, not by an agency or a hire.
